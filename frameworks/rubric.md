# Scoring rubric

Use this to score an answer by competency, at the level the candidate is interviewing for. Each score needs evidence: a short quote from the answer, or the absence of something the level expects. A score without a quote is a guess.

These anchors are this playbook's own synthesis of common PM interview practice. Companies weight competencies differently, so treat them as a consistent yardstick, not as any company's exact criteria.

## Scale

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Missing or wrong. The answer skips this or gets it wrong in a way an interviewer would mark down. |
| 2 | Present but thin. It's there, but generic, unjustified or shallow for the level. |
| 3 | Solid for the level. Clear, justified, and specific to this product and user. |
| 4 | Strong for the level. Adds insight an interviewer would remember: a sharp trade-off, a non-obvious segment, a metric that catches a real failure mode. |

## Competencies

Score only the competencies the question tests (the framework's "what it tests" section says which). Most questions test three to five.

| Competency | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Jumps to solutions; no visible plan | States a plan but drifts from it | Signposts a plan and follows it; the listener always knows where they are | Structure fits the question rather than a template, and adapts when an assumption changes |
| **User and problem insight** | Users are "everyone"; no problem stated | Names a segment without saying why | Picks a segment for a stated reason and names a specific, painful problem | Finds a segment or need others miss, with evidence or a sharp argument |
| **Prioritization and trade-offs** | Picks without criteria | Criteria named but not applied | Applies explicit criteria and says what's given up | Weighs second-order effects (cannibalization, trust, cost to serve) and changes the pick when they matter |
| **Metrics and analytics** | No success measure, or vanity metrics | A metric without a goal it ties to | A goal metric plus counter-metrics or guardrails, tied to the product's goal | Anticipates how the metric could mislead and designs around it |
| **Creativity** | Only obvious ideas | A few ideas, little range | Several ideas across different approaches, then narrows | At least one idea that reframes the problem, and it survives the trade-off analysis |
| **Business judgment** | Ignores the company's goals and constraints | Mentions the business without connecting it | Ties the recommendation to the company's strategy and economics | Spots a strategic risk or opportunity that changes the recommendation |
| **Communication** | Hard to follow; rambles | Clear in parts | Concise, checks in with the interviewer, lands a recommendation | Crisp summary up front, handles pushback without losing the thread |
| **Self-awareness** (behavioral) | No reflection | Generic lesson ("I learned to communicate") | A specific lesson tied to what they'd do differently | Shows changed behavior later, with an example |

## What changes by level

The same score means more at higher levels. Adjust expectations, not the scale.

| Level | Expect at a 3 |
|---|---|
| **APM / entry** | Clear structure, a sensible user and problem, a reasonable metric. Strategy can be light. |
| **PM** | All of the above, plus explicit trade-offs and guardrail metrics. |
| **Senior PM** | Business judgment and second-order effects without prompting; the recommendation is defended, not just stated. |
| **Group PM / Director** | Frames the problem at portfolio or company level, sequences bets, and says how they'd align teams and leadership. |

## Writing the score

For each competency scored: the score, one quote or the missing element as evidence, and what a 3 (or a 4) would have sounded like for this question. End with the single change that would raise the overall answer most.
